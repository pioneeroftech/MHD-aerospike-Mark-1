MARKINOFF · CH.01
FIELD MANUAL · 01 — THE MATH YOU CANNOT SKIP

Vectors, and the cross product that runs this whole engine

A number like "50" is a scalar — temperature, mass, your patience. A vector is a number that also cares about direction: velocity, force, magnetic field. Fifty newtons pointing at the wall and fifty newtons pointing at the sky are the same size and completely different outcomes. Ignore direction and you will build a beautiful machine that shoves itself sideways into a bulkhead. People have.

Two vectors can be multiplied two ways. The dot product asks "how much do these point the same way?" and hands you a scalar. Useful, not our problem today. The one that runs this engine is the cross product, written A × B. It eats two vectors and produces a third one perpendicular to both — sticking straight out of the plane they lie in.

|A × B| = |A| · |B| · sin θ

The size of the result depends on sinθ, the angle between the two inputs. Line them up (θ = 0°) and the cross product is zero — nothing. Set them at a right angle (θ = 90°, sinθ = 1) and it's maximum. Everything in between is a fraction you're leaving on the table. Remember this number; it's about to decide whether your thruster makes 40 N or 28 N for the same power.

Direction comes from the right-hand rule: point your fingers along the first vector, curl them toward the second, and your thumb points where the result goes. Do it with your actual hand. I'll wait. This isn't optional trivia — the direction your plasma gets thrown is set by exactly this gesture.

In the Lab: switch to Engineering mode and drag the FIELD ANGLE θ slider. Watch thrust die as you move away from 90°. That collapse is sinθ. You're feeling a cross product with a slider.
MARKINOFF · CH.02
FIELD MANUAL · 02 — WHERE THE PUSH COMES FROM

From a single charge to a river of plasma

A stationary electric charge sitting in a magnetic field feels nothing. Boring. But a charge moving through a magnetic field gets shoved sideways. That shove is the Lorentz force, and it is the seed of everything here.

F = q · (v × B)

Force on one charge q moving at velocity v through field B. There's your cross product again — the force is perpendicular to both the motion and the field. This is the entire reason chapter 01 wasn't a waste of your time.

Now stop thinking about one lonely charge. A current is just a parade of charges marching down a conductor. Add them all up and the single-charge law becomes the law for a current-carrying conductor of length L:

F = I · (L × B) → F = B · I · L · sinθ

A wire carrying current I across a field B gets pushed. This is the motor principle — every electric motor on Earth is this equation spinning in a circle. We are not going to spin it. We're going to point it out the back.

Here's the move that makes it a rocket instead of a motor: delete the wire. Replace the solid conductor with a channel full of plasma — a gas so hot its electrons have torn free, making the gas itself conductive. Now the current flows through the fluid, the field cuts across it, and the Lorentz force pushes on the plasma directly. The stuff that gets shoved is your propellant, and it flies out the exhaust. In three dimensions this is written with current density J (current spread over the fluid), and the force per unit volume is J × B — the "body force" that engineers use to describe MHD accelerators.

In our channel the current crosses the electrode gap h, so L becomes h, and the whole thing collapses to the number the Lab computes:

F = B · I · h · sinθ

That's it. That's the thrust. Everything else in this manual is either (a) how to feed this equation or (b) how to stop it from destroying your hardware.

This configuration has a name — the crossed-field accelerator — and it is not my invention. NASA was firing these in electromagnets decades ago. You're standing on a very tall pile of other people's genius. Try not to embarrass it.

MARKINOFF · CH.03
FIELD MANUAL · 03 — THE WORKING FLUID

Plasma: why it has to be gas, and why it has to be hot

The Lorentz force needs current, and current needs something that conducts. Cold air does not conduct — try pushing amps through the room and you'll get nothing but disappointment. So we cheat physics into cooperating: we ionize the gas. Heat it hard enough (thousands of degrees) and electrons rip loose from their atoms. Now the gas is a churning soup of free electrons and positive ions — plasma — and plasma conducts electricity like a liquid metal.

How well it conducts is a single number: conductivity σ (sigma), in siemens per meter. Hotter, more ionized plasma → higher σ → current flows easily. Let it cool → σ collapses → your "conductor" becomes an insulator and the whole engine chokes. This is why real machines run brutally hot and sometimes cheat by seeding the gas with easily-ionized metals like cesium or potassium to prop up conductivity at lower temperatures.

Why do you care? Because σ is quietly the most important knob you're not paying attention to. It sets your resistance, which sets your heat, which sets whether you melt. Hold that thought — chapter 04 is where it detonates.

In the Lab: drop PLASMA CONDUCTIVITY σ from 300 to 100 and run. Same everything else, and suddenly the channel cooks itself. You just made your propellant a worse conductor and paid for it in heat. Now you understand why nobody runs a cold MHD thruster.
MARKINOFF · CH.04
FIELD MANUAL · 04 — THE ENEMY

Resistance, I²R, and the quadratic trap that will melt you

Your plasma conducts, but not perfectly. It resists. And its resistance follows the same law as any conductor:

R = h / (σ · w · L)

Resistance = (distance the current travels, h) ÷ (conductivity σ × the area it flows through, w·L). Skinny gap, high conductivity, big electrode face → low resistance. Simple.

Now the part that ends careers. Any current forced through any resistance dumps energy as heat. The power lost is:

P_heat = I² · R

Read the exponent. Heat scales with current squared. Double the current and you get four times the heat. But look back at chapter 02 — thrust only scales with current to the first power. Double the current, double the thrust, quadruple the heat. That mismatch is not a bug you can engineer away. It is the fundamental tension of the entire project.

Here's the trap, in numbers. You want 40 N. Lazy approach: leave the field weak (B = 0.3 T) and crank current until F hits 40. That takes I ≈ 2667 A, which dumps 74 kW of heat and melts your electrodes in about eleven seconds. Smart approach: raise the field to B = 0.8 T instead, and 40 N now needs only I = 1000 A — a mere 10 kW of heat. Same thrust. Field is cheap. Current is a loan shark.

This is the lesson MARKINOFF refuses to hand you in the Lab — you have to melt something first. Consider yourself spoiled that I wrote it down here.

In the Lab / Analysis: open the Analysis tab and watch the amber heat curve bend upward and leave the straight green thrust line in the dust. That curve is I². That's your enemy drawn to scale.
MARKINOFF · CH.05
FIELD MANUAL · 05 — THE BILL

Power, back-EMF, and the generator you accidentally built

Somebody has to pay for all this. To push current I through the plasma's resistance you need a voltage of I·R — Ohm's law, the oldest tax in electronics. But there's a second, sneakier charge on the bill.

Remember chapter 02: a conductor moving through a magnetic field generates a voltage. Your plasma is a conductor, and it's screaming down the channel at speed u through the field B. So it generates its own voltage — pointing against you. This is back-EMF, and it's the universe's way of reminding you that your accelerator, run backwards, is literally an electric generator.

V_back = B · u · h   →   P_elec = I · (I·R + V_back)

Total voltage you must supply = resistive part (I·R) + the back-EMF you have to overcome. Multiply by current for power. Notice most designs spend far more power shoving current through resistance (which becomes heat) than they get back as motion. That ratio is your efficiency, and early on it will be embarrassing.

The takeaway: raising current doesn't just cost you heat, it costs you watts — twice, because I·R climbs with current too. Every argument in this manual keeps pointing the same direction: buy thrust with field, not amps.

In the Lab: get to Stage 001-C. The power budget will punish exactly the brute-force build the heat limit already punished. Physics is not subtle about its preferences.
MARKINOFF · CH.06
FIELD MANUAL · 06 — MAKING IT A ROCKET

Thrust, mass flow, and why specific impulse is the number that matters

Force pushing on plasma is nice. Rockets care about a subtler thing: how much you speed up how much propellant. Thrust is the rate you throw mass times how fast you throw it:

F = ṁ · Δu   →   u_exhaust = u_in + F/ṁ

ṁ is mass flow (kg/s). For a given force, less mass flow means each kilogram leaves faster — higher exhaust velocity. That sounds great until you remember you carry all that propellant with you.

Isp = u_exhaust / g₀

Specific impulse — exhaust speed divided by 9.81 — is fuel economy for rockets. High Isp means you get more push per kilogram of propellant, which means you can go farther without hauling a tanker. This is the number that decides whether your engine reaches orbit or just makes an impressive noise. Electromagnetic thrusters chase high Isp precisely because they can fling mass faster than any chemical flame.

The tension: for a fixed force, dropping mass flow raises Isp (good) but demands you dump more energy per kilogram (harder). You don't get to maximize everything. Welcome to engineering, where every win is a trade.

MARKINOFF · CH.07
FIELD MANUAL · 07 — THE PART IN THE TITLE

The aerospike: a nozzle that refuses to pick an altitude

Here's the problem every rocket nozzle has and nobody tells you about. A bell nozzle is tuned for one outside pressure. At sea level the thick atmosphere squeezes the exhaust plume and you lose thrust; up high in near-vacuum the plume flares out uselessly. A conventional nozzle is optimal at exactly one altitude and mediocre everywhere else. Rockets fly through every altitude. See the issue.

The aerospike solves it by turning the nozzle inside out. Instead of exhaust flowing inside a bell, it flows down the outside of a central spike, and the atmosphere itself forms the outer boundary of the exhaust. When outside pressure changes, the plume boundary just adjusts on its own. The result: it stays near-optimal from the launchpad to space. Altitude compensation, for free, with no moving parts. That's why it's in the project title and not, say, "MHD bell nozzle."

Now the "MHD" part earns its place. The plasma accelerator from chapters 02–06 is the source — it energizes and pre-accelerates the exhaust electromagnetically before it ever reaches the spike. You're stacking two ideas: electromagnetic acceleration (throw the mass fast) and altitude-compensating expansion (waste none of it). The Lab currently models the accelerator stage — the hard, unforgiving heart. The spike geometry is the next build layer, and now you actually know what it's for instead of nodding along to a buzzword.

Yes, real aerospikes have their own nightmares — base heating, spike cooling, manufacturing. We'll get there. One catastrophe at a time.

MARKINOFF · CH.08
FIELD MANUAL · 08 — PUTTING IT TOGETHER

The tradeoff web: there is no "best" setting

You now know every lever. Here's the ugly truth: pull any one and three others move. This is why engineering isn't a puzzle with an answer — it's a negotiation.

↑ current → ↑ thrust, but ↑↑↑ heat (I²) and ↑↑ power. The expensive lever.
↑ field → ↑ thrust with no heat penalty — but ↑ magnet mass. The smart lever, until mass bites.
↑ conductivity σ → ↓ resistance → ↓ heat and ↓ power. Free, if you can keep the plasma hot.
↑ channel area (w·L) → ↓ resistance → ↓ heat. Geometry is a lever too.
↑ cooling → survives heat directly — but ↑ radiator mass, which hurts acceleration.
↓ mass flow → ↑ specific impulse — but demands more energy per kilogram.

The engineer's job is to find the corner of this web that satisfies all your requirements at once — thrust AND thermal survival AND power budget AND mass — not to maximize any single one. Project 001 walks you into this deliberately: Stage A ignores heat, Stage B adds it, Stage C adds power. By the end you're juggling all of them, which is the actual skill.

FIELD MANUAL · 09 — HOW REAL ENGINEERS WORK

The loop. Run it every single time.

Random guess-and-check is how amateurs waste a week. Here's the loop that clears Project 001 in three deliberate moves:

  1. Define. The job: accelerate plasma to make measurable thrust for an aerospike upper stage.
  2. Requirements. A: F ≥ 20 N. B: F ≥ 40 N, no melt. C: also ≤ 55 kW.
  3. Model. Use ch.02–06 to predict the thrust before you build. Non-negotiable.
  4. Design. Choose B, I, geometry. Start conservative.
  5. Simulate. Run the 15-second burn.
  6. Analyze. Prediction vs measurement. A big gap = a relationship you don't own yet.
  7. Debug. Find the dominant failure. Usually heat. Ask which lever caused it.
  8. Modify. Change one thing. Beat heat by trading current for field.
  9. Test. Rerun. Did the change do what you predicted? If not, your model is wrong — fix the model, not just the number.
  10. Validate. All requirements met at once? Cleared. Otherwise back to step 6.

Notice step 3 and step 9. If you can't predict what your change will do, you're not engineering, you're gambling with extra steps.

FIELD MANUAL · 11 — WHERE THE MODEL LIES

Honest limits (because a model you trust blindly will kill you)

This Lab is a 0-D lumped model: it treats the whole channel as one averaged point. Perfect for building the instincts in your head. Useless for a flight design, because it deliberately ignores:

• Hall effect — at strong fields current skews sideways instead of crossing cleanly; real rigs fight it with segmented electrodes.
• Spatial structure — no boundary layers, no field non-uniformity, no inlet effects.
• Ionization cost — σ is a free slider here; in reality keeping plasma conductive costs real energy you'd have to budget.
• Radiation & real cooling — the thermal model is one lump with linear cooling.
• Aerospike fluid dynamics — not simulated yet; ch.07 is concept, the spike math is a future layer.

That's what the fidelity levels are for. Concept and Engineering modes are here now — for intuition and design. A future High-Fidelity mode adds the terms above, and here's the important part: the lessons you learn now don't get thrown out, they get sharper. You're learning real physics at a resolution you can actually reason with. Anyone who hands you full Navier–Stokes on day one is showing off, not teaching.
